Epidemic Modeling Toolkit

Build your own SEIR epidemic curve, or compare against a real historical outbreak.

The SEIR model splits a population into four groups: Susceptible (not yet infected), Exposed (infected, but not yet contagious — the incubation period), Infectious (infected and able to spread the disease), and Recovered (no longer infectious). It extends the simpler SIR model by explicitly modeling that incubation delay.

That extra compartment matters most for diseases with a real incubation period relative to how fast an outbreak unfolds — for example measles (~10–12 days), COVID-19 (~5–6 days), chickenpox (~2 weeks), and mumps (~2–3 weeks). It fits less well for diseases that spread faster than a realistic incubation delay allows the model to represent — see the flu example below, where that mismatch shows up directly in the fitted parameters.

Real-world example: 1978 boarding-school flu outbreak

SEIR's beta and gamma fitted via scipy.optimize.curve_fit against the real "boys in bed" counts from a 1978 English boarding-school influenza outbreak.

Published influenza parameters: an incubation period of roughly 1–4 days (about 2 days on average — the fixed value used here), an infectious period of about 4–6 days, and a basic reproduction number (R0) typically between about 1.2 and 1.4 in community settings (still under 2 even for past pandemic strains).

Why the fitted R0 above is so much higher: the incubation rate (σ) is held fixed rather than estimated, and this particular outbreak's case count rose extremely fast — from 3 to 298 students in bed within 5 days. To reproduce that rise while stuck with a realistic ~2-day incubation delay, the fit has to inflate β far beyond a plausible transmission rate to compensate. The result fits the observed curve well numerically, but the individual fitted parameters — especially R0 — shouldn't be read as real biological estimates. This is a known, documented consequence of fixing one parameter while fitting the others, not a bug.

Data: "Influenza in a boarding school," British Medical Journal, 4 March 1978.
